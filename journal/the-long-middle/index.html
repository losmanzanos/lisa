The long middle of leaving a faith

Lisa Wallace, LPC · October 2, 2026

Almost everything written about faith deconstruction covers one of two moments: the crisis, or the arrival somewhere new. The middle gets skipped, which is unfortunate, because the middle is where people spend most of their time.

The middle is not believing it anymore and not being anything else yet. It's still knowing all the songs. It's the reflex to pray during turbulence and the flinch right after. It's your mother asking what church you've found and you saying you're still looking, because the true answer takes forty minutes and will hurt her.

People arrive in my office apologizing for being in this stage. They've read that deconstruction is supposed to lead somewhere, and they're embarrassed to have been in transit for three years.

I want to say clearly: that's not a failure of process. Belief systems aren't opinions you swap. They come bundled with community, a calendar, a vocabulary for suffering, and usually your family. Dropping the claim takes an afternoon. Rebuilding everything the claim was holding up takes much longer.

What helps, in my experience, is naming what you actually lost rather than arguing about what's true. Most people discover they miss specific things. Singing with other people. Having language for grief. A reason for the week to have a shape.

Those are addressable. Some people find them again inside a tradition, some outside it. But you can't go looking for them while you're still insisting the only question is whether any of it was real.
